What Is a Website Trust Score — and Can You Actually Trust It?

Online shopping fraud hit a record $12.5 billion in losses in 2024. A website trust score is a fast signal to tell you whether a site is safe before you buy. Here's what goes into one, where the number comes from, and what it won't catch.

Every few weeks, someone finds a site selling something they want at a price that seems plausible, checks out, and then discovers the product never ships, the seller vanishes, and disputing the charge is a several-week headache. According to the FTC's 2024 Consumer Sentinel Data Book, online shopping was the second most commonly reported fraud category in the United States, behind only imposter scams. The median loss per incident in 2024 was $497 — not a rounding error for most people.

A website trust score exists to make that pre-purchase judgment faster and more systematic. Instead of manually checking whether a domain is newly registered, whether the contact page has a real address, and whether the SSL certificate is valid, a trust score aggregates those signals into a single number. The utility is obvious. The limitations are worth understanding.

What Actually Goes Into a Trust Score

Trust scores vary by the tool generating them, but the underlying signals tend to fall into a few categories:

Domain and Registration Data

How old is the domain? A site registered three weeks ago selling luxury goods is a very different risk profile than one registered in 2011. WHOIS data (when not redacted by privacy services) also reveals whether the registrant's information is consistent with the claimed business identity. Brand-impersonation sites often use recently registered domains with slight misspellings — amaz0n-deals.com rather than amazon.com, for instance. A Menlo Security report found that just over half of browser-based phishing attempts in 2024 involved some form of brand impersonation.

SSL and HTTPS

Whether a site uses HTTPS is table stakes — almost every site does now, including fraudulent ones, since free certificates from Let's Encrypt removed cost as a barrier. HTTPS means the connection between your browser and the server is encrypted. It does not mean the site is legitimate or that the operator is who they claim to be. A trust score that weighs HTTPS heavily on its own is not telling you very much. One that checks the certificate issuer, age, and whether it matches the domain is more meaningful.

Blacklist and Threat Intelligence Feeds

Major security vendors (Google Safe Browsing, PhishTank, abuse.ch, and others) maintain lists of domains flagged for phishing, malware distribution, or confirmed fraud. A trust score that queries these feeds in real time is checking whether the site has already been caught doing something harmful. The limitation: newly created fraud sites won't appear on any list yet. This is why domain age matters — it fills the gap before a malicious site gets reported.

Technical Legitimacy Signals

Legitimate businesses tend to have certain technical characteristics: consistent DNS records, professional hosting infrastructure, functional contact pages, privacy policies that reference actual legal frameworks. Fraud sites often cut corners here — a contact page that lists no phone number, a privacy policy copied verbatim from a template with the original company's name still in it, or hosting on a free tier that's inconsistent with the claimed scale of the business. The tech stack itself can be a signal.

Review and Reputation Data

Some trust scoring systems pull from review aggregators — Trustpilot, Google Reviews, the BBB, Sitejabber. Volume and sentiment matter, but so does the distribution: a site with 200 five-star reviews posted in the same two-week window is a different signal than one with 200 reviews spread across three years with a realistic mix of ratings.

The Limitation Worth Knowing

No trust score catches every threat. A site with a high score that was created by a professional operation with aged domains, realistic reviews, and legitimate infrastructure can still be fraudulent. Trust scores are probabilistic filters, not guarantees.

What to Do With a Low Trust Score

A low score is a prompt to investigate further, not a definitive verdict. If SaaS Detective or another tool flags a site as low-trust, the right response depends on what drove the score:

  • New domain + no reviews: Check if the business exists elsewhere (LinkedIn, social media, a verifiable physical address). A genuinely new small business will usually have some other digital presence.
  • Blacklist hit: Don't buy. Full stop. A site that's been flagged for phishing or malware by Google Safe Browsing has a documented history of harm.
  • Suspicious payment options only: If the site only accepts wire transfers, cryptocurrency, or payment methods with limited buyer protection, the risk is entirely on you if something goes wrong.
  • Technical inconsistencies: A site claiming to be a major retailer but running on free hosting, or claiming European operations but showing US-only infrastructure, warrants skepticism.

A Note on Counterfeit Products Specifically

A trust score helps identify sites that may take your money and deliver nothing. It's less useful for a separate but common problem: sites that do deliver something, but not what was advertised. A Michigan State University study published in 2023 found that nearly 7 in 10 consumers were misled into purchasing counterfeit items online at least once in the prior year, most commonly on e-commerce platforms and social media. The seller in those cases might have a perfectly legitimate-looking site with real reviews — for the knockoff products they're actually shipping.

For counterfeit risk specifically, buying directly from brand-authorized retailers or the manufacturer's own site is the more reliable defense than any automated trust score.

The Bottom Line

A website trust score is a useful first filter — it surfaces the signals that are annoying to check manually and catches obvious fraud patterns before you enter payment information. It's not a substitute for reading the return policy, checking independent reviews, or using payment methods with buyer protection when the stakes are high. Used as one layer of due diligence rather than the only one, it meaningfully reduces your exposure to the kind of fraud that cost US consumers $12.5 billion in 2024 — 38% of whom, per the FTC, lost real money when they reported a fraud, up from 27% the prior year.

The fraudsters are getting more sophisticated. The tools to check their work are too.
