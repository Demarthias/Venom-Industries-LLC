How to Smell a Scam Website in About 10 Seconds Flat

No jargon, no lecture, no "always use strong passwords" filler. Just the actual, fast checklist for deciding whether a website deserves your credit card number.

You don't need a computer science degree to catch most scam storefronts. You need about ten seconds and the willingness to actually use them instead of clicking "buy now" on autopilot. Here's the order I'd check things in, fastest and most useful first.

1. Does the Checkout Match the Brand?

Look at the URL when you actually get to the payment page, not just the homepage. If you're buying from "Coastal Home Goods" and the checkout suddenly says something like global-fulfillment-hub.net, that's not automatically a scam — some legitimate stores use third-party checkout platforms — but it's a mismatch worth a second glance, especially paired with anything else on this list.

2. How Old Is the Domain, Really?

Not "how old does it feel from the branding," because branding is cheap and templates are excellent now. A quick WHOIS lookup (or a tool that does it for you) tells you the actual registration date. A site claiming to be a long-established, beloved brand that was registered a month ago is telling you something important about itself.

3. Is There an Actual Way to Contact a Human?

A real phone number, a real address, an email that isn't a Gmail account pretending to be a corporate inbox. Scam storefronts tend to skip this because customer service costs money and effort neither party involved intends to spend.

4. Are the Reviews Suspiciously Uniform?

Real review sections have some spread — a few three-star reviews about slow shipping, someone complaining about the color being slightly off. A page with two hundred identical five-star reviews, all posted in roughly the same week, in roughly the same tone, was probably not written by two hundred different humans on their own time.

5. Does the Price Make Any Sense?

Steep discounts happen and don't automatically mean fraud. But "80% off a name-brand item, ships from a store nobody's heard of, today only" is a genre, and it's not a subtle one once you've seen it a few times.

The Shortcut Version

Any single one of these on its own usually means nothing. Two or three of them stacked together on the same site is the actual signal. Scam sites rarely fail just one check — they tend to fail a cluster of them at once.

The Honest Trade-Off

Doing all five checks manually, every time, for every unfamiliar site you shop from, is not going to happen. Nobody's actually going to do that at scale, including the person writing this.
