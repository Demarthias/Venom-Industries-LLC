Every Website Is Naked and Doesn't Know It

Every website you've ever visited has been quietly telling on itself the entire time — what software runs it, who processes its payments, how it tracks you, all of it, sitting right there in the page. Nobody looks. Here's what happens when you do.

I want you to picture your own front door for a second. Behind it: your actual life, your bank statements, that one drawer nobody's allowed to open. Now picture a website's homepage as its front door. Most people assume there's a similar amount of privacy going on back there. There isn't. A website's homepage is more like a guy at a party who's had two drinks and will now happily tell you his salary, his ex's name, and what he actually thinks of his boss, unprompted, before you've even said hello.

Every single site you load is handing your browser a shopping list of instructions: load this script from this company, send this data to that server, render this checkout form using this payment processor's code. Your browser reads all of it. It just doesn't bother telling you, because normally you're busy trying to buy a lamp, not audit a stranger's business.

What a Website Actually Confesses

Open the page source on basically any commercial website — right-click, "View Page Source," feel powerful for a second — and you'll find a small autobiography. It's not written in English, but it's not exactly subtle either. A few examples of what gets accidentally admitted:

  • Who they trust with their customer data. A script loading from a domain ending in a customer-management software provider's name means that company's sales team is logging every form you fill out.
  • Who's watching you shop. Ad-tracking scripts from major ad networks mean your visit is being packaged up and sold as a signal to show you shoe ads for the next three weeks.
  • Who actually gets your card number. The checkout form usually hands off to a named payment processor, and that name tells you a lot — whether it's a company that vets its merchants, or one you've genuinely never heard of before in your life.
  • How long they've actually existed. Domain registration records don't lie about age the way a shiny homepage can. A site dressed up like an established brand that was registered three weeks ago is, at minimum, worth a second look.

None of this is hidden on purpose. It's just hidden by default, in the sense that nobody built a friendly window into it. The information is public. It's just written in a language most people never learned, on purpose, because most people have better things to do with their Tuesday.

Why This Matters More Than It Sounds Like It Should

The gap between "this data is technically public" and "an actual human can read it in two seconds" is where most bad decisions happen — buying from a sketchy storefront, trusting a company that's outsourcing your data to nine other companies, assuming a legit-looking site is legit because it has a nice font.

Why I Started Paying Attention to This

I'm not a cybersecurity researcher with a badge and a lanyard. I'm one guy who got tired of feeling slightly stupid every time I bought something from a website I'd never heard of and had absolutely no way to sanity-check beyond "the reviews section says nice things and reviews sections have never once been faked by anybody, ever." So I built a tool — SaaS Detective — that does the reading for you. Point it at a page, and in about two seconds it tells you what's running underneath, in plain English, and whether the checkout looks like something you can trust with your actual credit card.

I'm not going to pretend this makes you invincible. Sophisticated scams exist and always will. But most sites aren't sophisticated scams — they're either obviously fine or obviously off, and the "obviously off" part usually shows up in the tech stack way before it shows up in the copywriting. A checkout running an unfamiliar processor nobody's ever vetted, a domain that's a few weeks old dressed up as a ten-year-old brand, zero actual infrastructure behind a site that claims to ship worldwide — those are all things you can catch in seconds, if something's actually reading the label for you.

The Part Where I Tell You What to Actually Do

You don't need to learn to read code. That's the entire point of this. It reads the label so you don't have to squint at a padlock icon and hope for the best.

Every website is naked. Most of them just assume nobody's going to look. Look anyway.
