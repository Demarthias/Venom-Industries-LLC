I Snooped on 50 Random Websites for Fun. Here's What I Found.

No agenda, no client work, just me, a Saturday, and a browser extension pointed at a random assortment of websites — big retailers, tiny print-on-demand shops, a couple of local business sites — to see what was actually running underneath. It got weirder than I expected.

I want to be upfront that this wasn't rigorous science. I didn't control for anything. I just opened a pile of tabs spanning big-box retail, small independent shops, a few blogs, a couple of local service businesses, and looked at what each one was quietly running. Consider it less "research study" and more "nosy neighbor with a browser extension." Here's the tour.

Finding One: Old Code Never Actually Dies

A genuinely surprising number of sites — including some that looked polished and modern on the surface — were still loading JavaScript libraries that are, in internet years, roughly the age of a fine wine that has gone slightly off. Nothing was catastrophically broken. It's more that the internet has a lot of buildings with a fresh coat of paint on a foundation nobody's checked on since it was poured. If it works, nobody goes back to look, and that's true right up until the day it very much doesn't work.

Finding Two: Some Checkout Pages Are Wearing Six Coats

One small shop's checkout page — the actual page where you type your card number in — was loading trackers from half a dozen different advertising and analytics companies at once. Not the homepage. The checkout. The place where you're at your most vulnerable and least interested in being retargeted with ads for the thing you just bought. I'm not accusing anyone of malice here; it's much more likely nobody ever went back and audited what got bolted onto that page over the years. But it's a good reminder that "checkout page" and "quiet, minimal, focused" are not the default state of the modern web — they're something a business has to actively choose.

Finding Three: The Boring Sites Were Often the Best-Run Ones

The most refreshing finds were the plain, slightly unglamorous small business sites — a repair shop, a local service provider — running lean, fast, minimal stacks with nothing extraneous bolted on. No fifteen trackers, no bloated page weight, just a site that does its job and gets out of the way. Boring, in this specific context, was a compliment. It usually meant someone had made a series of small, sensible decisions instead of just accumulating tools over time and never removing any of them.

The Actual Takeaway

Every site is a running record of every decision anyone ever made about it, and almost nobody ever goes back to clean up the old ones. A tech stack is less like a blueprint and more like a junk drawer — you can tell a lot about how a business operates just by seeing what's still in there.

Finding Four: A Few Checkout Pages Made Me Nervous

Not scam-nervous, exactly, but "would I put my card in here" nervous. A couple of stores — otherwise fine-looking — were running on payment processors I didn't recognize, paired with domains that hadn't existed very long. Nothing conclusive. Just enough to make me want to look a little closer before buying, which is honestly the entire point of building something that looks a little closer for you automatically.

Why I Did This At All

Partly curiosity, and partly because I think most people have a vague, foggy sense that "websites run on stuff" without ever actually seeing what that stuff is. It's a bit like knowing a restaurant has a kitchen without ever being allowed to see it. Once you actually look, even briefly, it changes how you read every site you visit afterward — not in a paranoid way, just in a slightly more informed one.
