I Got Half-Scammed by a Website Once, and It Was Humbling

I make a living telling people how to spot a sketchy checkout. A while back I almost got taken by one myself, at 1am, half-asleep, buying something dumb. Nobody's immune. Here's exactly how it went down.

I'd seen an ad for a gadget — nothing exotic, the kind of thing that shows up in your feed after you've searched for its more expensive cousin twice. The site looked fine. Clean layout, a few reviews, a padlock (see: not that impressive on its own), and a price that was aggressive but not so aggressive it set off alarm bells. I was tired. I was one thumb-scroll away from just trusting the process. I typed in my card number.

Here's the part that actually saved me, and it wasn't cleverness — it was a habit. Right before hitting "place order," I glanced at the checkout URL out of pure muscle memory, the same way you check your mirrors even on a street you've driven a thousand times. The domain didn't match the brand on the page. Not wildly — no extra letters, nothing cartoonish — just a completely different name, one that looked like it belonged to a company that resells other people's storefronts wholesale. That's a real thing that exists, and it's not always a scam by itself, but paired with everything else, it was the first actual crack in the whole performance.

What I Did Next

I backed out and did the thing I should have done before I ever got to checkout: I looked at how old the domain was. Weeks. Not months — weeks. A store selling a "trusted, beloved" gadget brand that had apparently existed on the internet for less time than my houseplant had been alive. I checked the payment processor. It wasn't one I recognized, and a quick search on it turned up a small pile of other people describing versions of the exact same experience I was about to have.

I didn't lose any money. I lost about eleven minutes and a decent chunk of my ego, because I genuinely thought I was better at this than "almost bought a probably-fake gadget at 1am because the layout looked nice."

The Actual Lesson

A convincing website is not evidence of a legitimate business. It's evidence that someone knows how to use a website template. Those used to correlate a lot more than they do now — templates got good, scams got professional, and the visual bar for "looks trustworthy" dropped to nearly zero effort.

What I Actually Check Now

  • Does the checkout domain match the brand? Not a full audit — just a glance. Mismatches aren't always fraud, but they're always worth a second look.
  • How old is the domain, actually? Not "how old does it feel," which is a number your brain will happily make up to match a nice logo.
  • Do I recognize the payment processor? Recognizable processors have onboarding standards. Unfamiliar ones sometimes don't, and "sometimes" is doing real work in that sentence.

The honestly annoying part is that I know all of this. I built a tool specifically so people don't have to remember to check it manually at 1am when their guard is down — and I still almost skipped every step of it myself, because being tired is a more powerful force than expertise. So now I just run the check automatically, because willpower was never going to be the plan.
