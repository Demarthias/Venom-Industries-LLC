The Little Padlock Icon Is Lying to You (Kind Of)

You know the one. Little padlock, next to the address bar, right where your eyes go before you type in a card number. It's been quietly doing a much smaller job than everyone thinks for over a decade now.

Somewhere along the way, an entire generation of internet users got taught a shortcut: padlock good, no padlock bad. It's not wrong, exactly. It's just answering a much smaller question than the one you're actually asking. You're asking, "can I trust this website?" The padlock is only ever answering, "is the connection between my browser and this server encrypted?" Those used to overlap a lot more than they do now.

What the Padlock Actually Promises

The padlock means your connection to that specific server is encrypted — nobody sitting on the same coffee shop Wi-Fi as you can casually read the traffic between your browser and the site. That's genuinely useful. It's also table stakes. Getting a security certificate used to cost money and require some technical competence, which filtered out a lot of low-effort scam sites by accident. Then free, automated certificate issuance became standard practice across the web, and the filter disappeared. Now the padlock shows up on legitimate banks and on a site selling suspiciously cheap electronics that was registered two weeks ago. Both get the same little icon. Neither one is lying, technically. The icon just never claimed to vouch for anyone's character.

The One-Sentence Version

The padlock tells you the mailman can't read your letter. It tells you nothing about whether the person you're mailing it to is going to run off with your money.

So What Should You Actually Look At?

Good news: the signals that actually matter are also sitting right there in public, they're just less famous than a padlock icon. A few worth knowing:

  • Domain age. A store that's been around for six years has had six years to either build a reputation or get reported into oblivion. A store registered nineteen days ago has had nineteen days to do neither.
  • Who's actually processing the payment. Recognizable payment processors vet the businesses that use them. An unfamiliar processor you've never heard of, with no other signals backing it up, is a genuinely different risk category — not automatically a scam, but worth a second look.
  • Whether the rest of the site looks maintained. A working contact page, a return policy that isn't obviously copy-pasted from somewhere else with the wrong company name still in it, basic functioning infrastructure — legitimate businesses tend to sweat these details because they plan to still exist next year.

None of these require you to become a network security expert on a Tuesday afternoon. They just require something to check them for you, because nobody's opening a terminal before buying a phone case.

Where This Leaves You

I'm not telling you to distrust the padlock. I'm telling you it retired from its job as "the internet's trust signal" a while ago and nobody sent out an announcement. Treat it as the bare minimum, not the finish line. If a site clears the padlock and nothing else, that's not a compliment — that's just a website that exists.
